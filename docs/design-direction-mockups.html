Recommendation: Direction 2, Split Stack

The Core Experience principle established earlier treats seeing the map, seeing the full list, and moving the pin as equally critical — not a primary flow plus edge cases. Split Stack is the only direction that keeps map and list permanently, simultaneously visible without a gesture layer or a hidden-by-default state standing between the rider and either one.

It's also the cleanest fit for Progressive Enhancement (PRD Web App Requirements): map-block-then-list-block is a trivial server-rendered DOM order — if the map/JS layer is slow or fails, the list still renders in place, no broken drag-sheet or dead "ver en mapa" button. Direction 1 (bottom sheet) is the most polished-feeling but needs real gesture-handling JS, disproportionate complexity for a solo, no-build stack. Direction 3 undersells the map-first identity that's core to the brief. Direction 4 hides full route detail behind a chip carousel, in tension with "always show the full nearby picture, never a single answer." Direction 5 isn't a competing choice — it's how Direction 2 naturally extends once there's desktop width to spare.

Open questions, resolved

Three gaps surfaced while reviewing the chosen direction: what the displayed time actually means, how visually-overlapping routes render, and what a bus stop looks like. All three are now folded into Direction 2/5 above (the mockups already reflect the fix) and explained here.

1. Walk time + bus ETA, in plain language

Not icon+number pairs, but a sentence a knowledgeable friend would actually say: "Llega en X min · Y min caminando." A comfortable margin needs nothing further. A tight one gets an honest, action-oriented close: sal ya (leave now) or no alcanzas (you won't make it) — still shown, never hidden. Both times derive from the stop's already-known straight-line distance, not a new routing dependency.

2. Overlapping / parallel routes — bundled, not stacked

Where two or more routes share the same physical corridor for a stretch (real for TM trunk lanes), each line offsets a few pixels perpendicular to the shared path so every corridor color stays visible as thin parallel strokes — the standard transit-cartography fix (Citymapper, official metro diagrams), not a novel invention. Routes that only cross at a point need no offset; only genuinely parallel shared segments do.

3. Stop markers — station vs. paradero vs. "your location"

The halo-ring station marker turned out to sit too close, visually, to the "you are here" pin — both were circular dots with a ring/border treatment. Fixed by giving location a genuinely different silhouette, not just a color: a teardrop pin, the universally-recognized "this point was chosen" shape, anchored at its point rather than its center. Circles stay reserved for the network itself (stops), the teardrop is reserved for the one point that's about the rider. TM stations keep the halo-ring — a larger dot with a ring around it, the "interchange station" convention from real metro diagrams. SITP paraderos keep the plain small dot. Neither stop type defaults to a route's color — a stop can serve many routes at once, so at rest it stays neutral with a small route-count badge, taking on a route's color only once that route is selected.
